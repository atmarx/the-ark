# Cartographer's Mercy

*A design brief written with Claude (Anthropic) while designing the Ark postcards.  It's kept as a record of what the designs were aiming for.*

A design philosophy for small printed objects that want to be found, not
delivered.  For the thing you slip into a book you return to a library, or
tuck under the windshield wiper of a stranger whose bumper sticker you agreed
with.  For the card that looks like it was always there.

## The movement

Cartographer's Mercy is the aesthetic of the field journal left behind in a
drawer — surveyor's grids, tide tables, anonymous hermits' almanacs.  It
borrows its authority not from the pulpit but from the instrument: the ruled
margin, the hand-corrected measurement, the footnote written in a margin too
narrow for it.  Every decision was made by someone who had to carry their kit
up a mountain.

The movement rejects the bright, the shouted, the optimized.  It takes its
cues from the practical traditions of mapmaking, ship's logs, naturalists'
plates, weather records and almanacs.  Information is preserved here, not
pitched.  If the object survives being folded into a back pocket for three
weeks, it has done its job.

## Surface and space

The ground is cream or oat, never pure white.  The ink is singular — one
color, one pass — an oxblood, an iron gall blue-black, a deep field teal. The
single-ink rule is strict.  Screens and gradients are out, because the tool
that prints this must be able to be a 1970s duplicator or a thrift-store
laserjet and the work must not know the difference.  Margins are wide and
ruled in fine hairlines, the way a ledger rules its columns.

Grid lines are legible but quiet, 0.25pt hairlines at most.  The composition
observes a modular baseline grid, and the eye can feel this even when it
cannot name it.  Alignments are optical, not merely mathematical; a
craftsman's hand has nudged what the algorithm would have left flush.

## Mark and letter

Typography is the voice of the movement.  The headline is a letterpress-era
slab or condensed antique: Clarendon, Caslon Black, Stymie.  It is set
generously tracked, in small caps where appropriate, with the kerning massaged
by hand one pair at a time.  Body text is a patient text serif — a Garamond, a
Bell, a Caslon — at a size that respects the reader's middle years.  Numerals
are old-style.  The ampersand is a flourish.

Mark-making is restrained and purposeful: a compass rose no bigger than a
thumbprint; a dingbat leaf; a rule that ends in a small serif hook.  A single
figurative mark is permitted per composition, hand-drawn in the same ink,
reproduced at the scale of a wax seal.

## Weathering and authority

The final artifact looks as if it has been folded once and carried in a breast
pocket.  Not distressed — never distressed, never fake-aged — but specific:
the corner nicked where the card caught on a thumbnail, the ink darker at the
edge of a letterform where the press held too long.  These marks do not appear
in the digital master; they appear in the printing, in the paper, in the
knife-cut edge.  The design reserves room for them.

Authority in Cartographer's Mercy comes from understatement.  A coordinate
printed in the lower right corner — even a fictional one — grants more
credibility than any headline.  The card claims nothing it cannot prove with
an instrument.  It names the year and the hand that made it, and nothing more.

## The reading order

There is one dominant element — usually a headline or a single glyph — and
then a quiet descent into the body.  The eye enters at the top-left rule,
travels the headline, falls through the body in a narrow measure (no wider
than an alexandrine), and exits at a small locator in the lower right: a URL,
a coordinate, a date in roman numerals.  Nothing competes.  Nothing shouts.
Hierarchy is achieved by weight and space, never by color or size alone.

## Craft

This is slow work.  Every rule is drawn where a rule belongs.  Every glyph is
placed where the grid and the eye agree.  The tracking of the headline has
been looked at on paper, not only on screen.  The ink density has been
considered against the paper stock.  The object, when held, feels like it was
made by someone who has made a thousand of them.

The result should not announce its craftsmanship.  It should merely refuse to
look like anything else on the table.
