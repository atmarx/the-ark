# Concrete Poetry

*A design brief written with Claude (Anthropic) while designing the Ark postcards.  It's kept as a record of what the designs were aiming for.*

A design philosophy for printed objects that want to be read as shapes before
they are read as sentences.  For the card that announces itself by its
silhouette across a room, and rewards the approach with a second, quieter
reading close-up.  For the artifact that could hang in a gallery and could
also be taped inside a toolbox without anyone noticing the difference.

## The movement

Concrete Poetry is the descendant of the Swiss school and the mid-century
visual-poetry tradition — Gomringer, Finlay, the Bauhaus type labs, the Ulm
grotesques, the early-eighties Basel posters.  The form of the text is the
meaning of the text.  A word sized by its weight in the world.  A numeral that
is the subject of the page, not its ornament.  Arrangement itself is a kind of
argument.

The movement rejects ornament for its own sake — the drawn flourish, the
decorative rule, the sepia memory of a letterpress shop.  It prefers the
neutrality of the grotesque, the unblinking numeral, the unsentimental rule.
The object earns its authority by refusing to decorate, by trusting the reader
to read the shape before the words.

## Surface and space

The ground is pure white, uncoated, indifferent.  The ink is black — a single
pass, saturated, unforgiving.  No screens, no gradients, no accent color.  The
single-ink constraint is identical to Cartographer's Mercy but for opposite
reasons: there, to respect the cheap duplicator; here, to refuse any softening
of the signal.  Margins are generous and empty — the white space is
structural, a pressure that shapes where the type sits.

Rules are hairline (0.25pt) and rare.  A rule exists only to register the top
and bottom of the composition — a Swiss header bar, a Swiss footer bar — and
it lands exactly at the baseline or x-height of a numeral already on the page.
Nothing is drawn that a letterform could have drawn instead.

## Mark and letter

Typography is the entire composition.  The display face is a grotesque of
measured weight — Inter Black, Helvetica Neue Black, Akzidenz — set at display
sizes large enough that the counters and terminals become the primary figures
on the page.  The body face is the same family at its text weight.  Numerals
are tabular and monospaced — JetBrains Mono, IBM Plex Mono, DIN — set at the
smallest size the reader can still resolve.  The contrast between one huge
grotesque numeral and a column of monospace micro-figures is the whole grammar
of the style.

Tracking is tight where the type is large (negative letterspacing at display
sizes) and open where the type is small (positive at 6pt and below).  Caps are
preferred over caps-and-lowercase at the small sizes because the x-height of a
grotesque at 5.6pt is unreliable on a home laser printer.

## Type as data

The central move of this movement: the arrangement of the type carries the
data.  A word's size is proportional to its weight in the inventory. A numeral
sized 142pt says something its 12pt cousin cannot.  A stacked column whose
leading tightens from top to bottom implies acceleration. The reader takes the
shape of the page as information before they read the page as language.

This is the rule that separates concrete poetry from mere minimalism: **the
composition must argue something by its form**, or it is not earning the
constraint.

## Weathering and authority

The final artifact looks as if it has been printed that morning.  No folds, no
stains, no fake-aged paper.  The authority is contemporary and unmediated —
the opposite of the Cartographer's pocket-worn credibility. The card claims
the present tense.  It says: this is how large the problem is, right now, and
this is how much of the solution fits on the drive in your hand, right now.

The edges are knife-cut, square, and clean.  If the object survives being
pinned to a corkboard for a year, it has done its job — the print will be
exactly as sharp as the day it was cut.

## The reading order

The eye lands on the hero numeral first — unavoidable, it takes the page. Then
the eye jumps to the right-hand inventory, where the type is sized by volume;
the reader learns without reading that Gutenberg is the largest category by a
wide margin.  Then down to the subtitle, which resolves the argument in three
short lines.  Then out via the Swiss footer bar.  The reading is fast by
design — under three seconds for the full composition, twenty for the full
inventory.

## Craft

The craft is in the restraint.  No secondary color could improve this object;
no illustration could clarify it; no ornament could lend it authority it does
not already carry.  The grotesque, the hairline, the numeral — these are the
entire toolkit.  The page is a demonstration that the tools are sufficient.

The result should look as if it could be on a museum wall, a thumbtack board,
or a hand-addressed envelope, and be equally correct in all three contexts.
